## Question, and what is and is not estimated

Does a utility that takes on material data center load charge its residential customers more afterward than it otherwise would have? The planned water analysis could not be completed because adequate historical rate coverage was not obtained.

**The estimand, electricity.** The additional change in residential price at utilities that became data center hosts in 2011 to 2022, relative to low-load utilities in the same market region, through three years.

It is not the total effect of data centers on bills. Region-wide wholesale and capacity costs fall on hosts and comparison utilities alike and are differenced out. It is not a pure local-grid-cost effect either, because procurement and pass-through differ between utilities in the same region. It does not cover northern Virginia or the other long-time hosts: 32 utilities were already above the load threshold before 2011 and are excluded. It does not cover the utilities that first cross in 2023 or later. On the design as it stands (Inference) there are 41 cohort utilities at 2%, 7 of them in the headline set. On the inventory as built (read 2026-11-19, D-102; `data/frozen/prefreeze_quantity_summaries.json`, `open_item_13`; provisional, Inference) there are 21 late hosts at 2%. Of the inventory's 2025 data center load, the long-time hosts carry 49.5%, the cohort utilities 36.3%, of which the headline set carries 9.8 points, the late hosts 10.8%, and utilities that never reach the threshold 3.4%. These are read again from the final inventory at the freeze. The write-up shows how these three groups differ.

The author's stated prior is "mostly no, sometimes yes under identifiable conditions." The plan is written so that prior can lose. An average over these hosts cannot by itself establish "mostly no": a small average does not show that most utilities have small effects.
