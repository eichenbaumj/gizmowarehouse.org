# Prior work: what has been estimated, and what is left

Verified 2026-11-11; four papers that had been out of reach were read in full the same day from PDFs downloaded by hand. Full record per study in [prior-work-detail.md](prior-work-detail.md); quotes, blocked projects and polling in [claims.md](claims.md). "Primary" means we read the number in the paper. "Secondhand" means press or a summary only; those numbers are not quotable yet.

## The short version

Electricity has been studied a lot, quickly. At least ten 2026 working papers estimate what data centers did to retail prices. Six of them use a utility-level panel, which is the design we planned. One is peer reviewed (Feng and You, Nature Energy, 9 November 2026, read at abstract level only). Almost all rely on purchased facility data, and none has posted code or data.

For residential customers most verified estimates are small. Apart from one paper, the utility-level and county-level estimates of the average effect fall between about -1.5% and +2.1%, and the one state-level estimate is negative. The exception is Scalera, Bosetti and Pecci: about 1.1% to 1.3% per facility, which the authors headline as 13% per ten facilities. That estimate falls by 40% when the largest territories are not capped. The papers do not agree on where the effect shows up. Two find it at investor-owned utilities, one finds it only at co-ops and municipal utilities, and they split on restructured markets as well. The loud numbers in public debate come from somewhere else: wholesale prices, capacity auctions, and forward projections.

Water has not been studied. We tried to disprove that and could not: there is no multi-utility, after-the-fact statistical study of what data centers did to water rates or bills. Confidence is moderate, because some sources could not be opened.

Not retail, but the source of the big public numbers:

- **Wholesale.** Kay, Reaser, Taylor (Dallas Fed WP 2606, read in full) model dispatch in 23 regions: data center load raised wholesale prices 6.9% nationally in 2025 and 15.0% in northern Virginia, with the Pacific Northwest slightly higher at 15.4%. Effects spread to regions with few data centers.
- **PJM capacity.** The market monitor's figures are counterfactual simulations, not measurements. For the 2028/29 auction its October 2026 report gives $6.27 billion (38%) if all data center load is removed and $5.73 billion (35%) if only forecast growth is removed. These are two scenarios, not competing estimates. E3's own analysis for the Data Center Coalition attributes about half of the 2024/25 to 2025/26 price jump to load growth.
- **State cross-sections.** Berkeley Lab finds states with more load growth saw lower all-sector prices, but for residential prices alone the relationship is smaller and not significant (-0.22, SE 0.12).

## What this does to our plan

1. **A plain utility-level panel would duplicate Meeks et al. and Bahar and Wright.** Our electricity work earns its place only as the open reconciliation: same public outcome data, a facility inventory anyone can rebuild, and a table that walks from one paper's answer to another's by changing one choice at a time. Lange et al. already use the same journalist-built permit set we start from, so "open facility data" alone is not new either; hand-verified serving utilities, sourced start years and a load measure are.
2. **We differ from the closest paper in three checkable ways.** Meeks et al. code every utility whose territory touches the data center's county as treated; we assign the one utility that serves the site and verify the large ones by hand. They price restructured-state customers at the default-service rate only; we add the competitive supply side. They stop at 2024; we have 2025.
3. **The average is roughly agreed; the conditions are not.** Before the last four papers were read, the disagreement looked like a pattern: positive at investor-owned utilities and in restructured states, nothing elsewhere. That reading does not survive them.
   - *Ownership.* Meeks et al. and Scalera et al. find the effect at investor-owned utilities and almost none elsewhere. Ryu and Hiatt find the reverse: co-ops and municipal utilities, nothing at investor-owned. Bahar and Wright find nothing at hyperscale hosts of any kind.
   - *Retail choice.* Meeks et al. find a larger effect in restructured states (+4.0% against +0.7%). Scalera et al. find a smaller one (44% less, weakly estimated).
   - *Who built it.* Rana et al. find the effect at third-party sites and not at Big Tech sites, but cannot statistically separate the two (p = 0.10). Bahar and Wright's colocation and hyperscale estimates are both indistinguishable from zero.
   - *Slack.* Scalera et al. find smaller effects where the utility had spare capacity. Rana et al. find, weakly, smaller effects where utilization was higher. These point in opposite directions.

   So "mostly no, sometimes yes" still describes the averages, but nobody has established the "sometimes." Our four pre-set conditions test exactly the splits these papers disagree on, which is a better reason to run them than agreement would have been.
4. **Water is where the new evidence is.** Everything cited for water today is engineering projection, one bond-market study, or single-town reporting.
5. **Two of the four newly read papers lean on shortcuts we have already measured in our own inventory.** These are concrete rows for the one-change-at-a-time table.
   - Scalera et al. assign each facility to the smallest overlapping service-territory polygon, with no check against the utility that serves the site. Our hand verification found that rule wrong for about one large site in four even after a load-feasibility screen (D-3, D-8). A site wrongly given to a small municipal or co-op utility is also a candidate explanation for their ownership split.
   - Lange et al. use the Business Insider permit set and date entry by the first permit issued in a county. When we checked that kind of date against public sources for large sites it was within two years 44% of the time (D-10). Their treatment is any facility of any size, with no load measure.
   - Ryu and Hiatt also assign by territory polygon, and Rana et al. take the vendor's utility field as given. None of the four reports validating the serving utility.
6. **Our 2025 claim, restated.** Ryu and Hiatt's annual panel ends in 2024; their 2025 figure adds preliminary monthly data that is weighted toward large utilities. Rana et al. reach 2025 with monthly data for 42 utilities. Lange et al. say their county prices run through 2025 in a draft dated April 2026; I am uncertain what 2025 file that could have been, since the annual survey for 2025 was not final by then. We should say "the full annual survey for 2025," not "the first to 2025."
7. **One paper still unread:** Feng and You (Nature Energy, 9 November 2026), abstract only. It is behind a $40 paywall and has not been bought.
